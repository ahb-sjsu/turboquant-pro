\begin{abstract}
We measure routed inverted-file search over one trillion 4-bit quantized vectors against the exact
scan of the same compressed index. The index is 500 servers of two billion rows, 24 TB, built from
a seeded synthetic corpus on the National Research Platform's shared Kubernetes cluster. Probing 32
of 2048 cells, routed search returns 0.989 of the exact top ten for queries that are corpus rows
and 0.969 for queries the index does not contain, and probing 128 cells it returns 0.999 for both.
PENDING-1TRG-ABSTRACT. Within the trillion-row index, subsets of servers that differ only in their
number of rows show no loss of recall as rows are added, and a gain at narrow widths. Because a
probed cell is scored exactly as the scan scores it, recall at any width is the share of reference
neighbours in the probed cells, and that share, computed from cell metadata, matched every measured
width, three of them measured after it was recorded. Holding recall at 32 probes costs a median of
19 billion rows scored per query, 1.9 percent of the index, on all 500 servers. Against float rows
regenerated from the seed the compressed ranking keeps at most 0.621 of the true neighbours, so the
result is about routing and not about compression. We describe the jobs, the pool and the
accounting of the run, and every number can be recomputed from the archived record.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Approximate nearest-neighbour search over compressed vectors is routine at a billion rows
\cite{jegou2011product,johnson2019faiss,subramanya2019diskann,chen2021spann}, and indexes of a
trillion vectors have been built. The Faiss library paper \cite{douze2024faiss} describes an
index of 1.5 trillion vectors built in 2000 shards on a few hundred servers and searched in about a
second per query, and UBASE \cite{tian2026ubase} serves a production deployment of about a trillion
vectors. Neither reports recall at that scale. UBASE reports recall on sets of up to about a hundred
million vectors and latency and memory at a trillion, and the Faiss account reports the build and
the search time. What is missing at that scale is the comparison that defines the error of routing,
the routed result against an exact scan of the whole index.

This paper makes that comparison at $10^{12}$ rows. When an inverted-file index is partitioned
into shards that share one coarse quantizer and one rotation, and the corpus grows by adding
shards, does the routing lose anything it would have found by scanning everything? We built the
whole index, scanned all of it exactly, ran the routed search over the same shards at five probe
widths, and compared. Three things make the comparison more than one number. The exact scan of any
subset of servers is the merge of that subset's partial results, so one index gives a scale series
in which only the number of rows changes. Recall at any width follows from where the reference
neighbours sit in the router's probe order, so the curve over widths can be computed from metadata
and checked against routed passes. And the corpus is a seed, so every row can be regenerated and
every number recomputed from the record.

The findings are these. Routed recall against the exact scan is 0.989 at 32 probes and 0.999 at
128 probes at $10^{12}$ rows for queries that are corpus rows, with 95 percent bootstrap intervals
over the 100 queries of 0.982 to 0.995 and 0.997 to 1.000, and 0.969 and 0.999 for 100 queries the
index does not contain (Section~\ref{sec:results}). PENDING-1TRG-INTRO. Inside the trillion-row
index recall moves by less than 0.01 at 128 probes as servers are added and rises at 16 and 32
probes (Section~\ref{sec:nested}). The recall curve over nine widths computed from the cell ranks of
the reference neighbours matched the five widths measured, three of them measured after the curve
was recorded (Section~\ref{sec:reach}). Holding that recall costs a fixed share of the index per
query, a median of 19 billion rows at 32 probes (Section~\ref{sec:work}). The limit is that the
corpus is synthetic, of intrinsic dimension about 16, and the reference is the compressed index's
own exact scan. Section~\ref{sec:limits} gives the figures that bound the result, an upper bound of
0.621 on the compressed ranking's true recall at $10^{12}$ rows and a real-embedding pilot.

The index was built and measured on the National Research Platform's Nautilus cluster
\cite{weitzel2025nrp}, which many research groups share and on which nothing is reserved. Every
build job and almost every measurement job ran in the cluster's smallest job class, one CPU and two
gigabytes. Section~\ref{sec:run} describes how, and Section~\ref{sec:cost} accounts for what the run
used.

\paragraph{Index.} Each row is a 32-dimensional vector projected to 24 dimensions by one basis
shared by the whole fleet, quantized to 4 bits per dimension, and assigned to one of 2048
inverted-file cells by one shared coarse quantizer. Both the basis and the quantizer are fitted
once, on a bootstrap shard, and read by every build job. A shard holds five million rows. A server
holds 400 shards, two billion rows, on one block volume. The on-disk row is 12 bytes of codes, 8
bytes of per-row norms used by the asymmetric distance computation, and 4 bytes of cell membership.
Every server reports 48\,009\,656\,624 index bytes for its two billion rows, 24.0 bytes per row,
so the fleet holds 24.0 TB of index, on volumes provisioned at 56 GiB each, 30.1 TB in all.
Scoring is asymmetric distance computation over the codes, in row blocks, from memory-mapped
shards. Because the basis and the coarse quantizer are shared, scores from different shards are
comparable and a global top-$k$ is the exact merge of per-shard top-$k$s (Figure~\ref{fig:fleet}).
That property is what makes the measurement below exact rather than approximate.

\paragraph{Routing.} A query is rotated by the shared basis and compared with the 2048 cell
centroids. Each cell gets an upper bound on the cosine any of its rows can have with the query,
from the angle to its centroid less half the cell's angular radius, and the routed search scores
every row of the $p$ cells with the highest bound, on every server, with the same asymmetric
distance the exact scan uses. The radii are the bootstrap's, so every server probes the same cells
for a query.

\paragraph{Corpus.} The corpus is defined by a seed and regenerated on demand. Each global shard
draws a fresh rank-16 Gaussian basis, projects Gaussian coefficients through it with a linear decay
of singular values from 1.0 to 0.3, and adds five percent noise, in an ambient dimension of 32. A
shard is a pure function of its global index, so any job can produce any shard without reading
anything, and the build moved no corpus bytes. A build from files would first have placed 128 TB of
float vectors where 500 jobs could read them. A single shard is rank 16 plus noise by
construction. Across shards the intrinsic dimension measures 15.4 to 17.0 by the two-nearest-
neighbour estimator \cite{facco2017twonn} on unit-normalized samples of 25000 to 200000 rows drawn
across shards, with no trend whether the rows grow within a fixed set of shards or as 5 to 40 new
shards. These are mixed-shard estimates, and no within-shard estimate was made. Real embeddings such as Cohere Embed v3 at
1024 dimensions measure 52 to 61 by the same estimator. Hubness, the concentration of
nearest-neighbour lists on a few points \cite{radovanovic2010hubs}, rises with corpus size on this
recipe, at about 0.42 per decade at $k=10$ against roughly 0.25 per decade on real data. We
attribute the rise to how shards are added. Each shard draws its own random 16-dimensional subspace,
two random 16-dimensional subspaces of a 32-dimensional space are not orthogonal, and so the rows of
a new shard land close to rows of shards already present. Whatever its cause, the rise does not act
on the quantity measured here, which depends only on whether the router reaches the cell of each
reference neighbour (Section~\ref{sec:reach}).

\paragraph{Scale points.} The same protocol has been run at $10^{8}$ rows on 4 servers, $10^{9}$
on 4, $10^{10}$ on 8, $10^{11}$ on 50 and $10^{12}$ on 500. Per-server work is constant from
$10^{11}$ on, two billion rows, so the step from 50 to 500 servers changes nothing per node and
changes only the number of chances to draw a slow or failing one.

A measurement is a set of phases, each a pool of independent jobs (Table~\ref{tab:phases}). The
query-cache phase writes the query set to the shared volume once, and every server reads it from
that file. The reference phase is one job per server that scans the server's two billion rows
exactly and writes its top-10 per query with scores. The routed phase is one job per server that
runs the routed search at 32 and 128 probes and writes the same shape of partial. The score phase
merges the 500 reference partials into the exact global top-10, merges the routed partials the
same way, and reports recall at ten of the routed set against the reference set, averaged over
queries. A second routed sweep at 16, 64 and 256 probes was submitted the day the first score
landed and scored two days later, and two metadata phases read only the partials and the cell
metadata (Section~\ref{sec:reach}). The score program has not changed since the $10^{9}$ run.

Three query sets were measured. The corpus-row set is the first 25 rows of each of four seeded
shards, 0, 50000, 100000 and 150000, spread evenly across the index, and the same recipe gave the
queries at every earlier scale point, so the comparison across sizes holds the query recipe fixed.
The non-member set is the first 25 rows of each of shards 200000, 250000, 300000 and 350000 of the
same generator, seeds no corpus shard uses, so no query is in the index. Both draw from four
shards, and each shard has its own subspace, so both cover four subspaces of the generator. The
many-shard set covers two hundred. It is row 0 of each of shards 400000 to 400099, none in the
index, and row 0 of one shard on each of 100 servers spread across the index, all in it. It was
measured after the index volumes were released, on an index regenerated from the seed
(Section~\ref{sec:regen}).

\subsection{Recall across four orders of magnitude}

Table~\ref{tab:scale} and Figure~\ref{fig:scale} give the number the run exists to produce. At
$10^{12}$ rows, routed search at 128 probes of 2048 cells returns 0.999 of the exact scan's top
ten, and at 32 probes 0.989. At 128 probes the figure is 0.999, 0.9988 and 0.9986 at the three
smaller scales, a spread of 0.0004. At 32 probes it rises slowly with scale, 0.979, 0.982, 0.983,
0.989, and Section~\ref{sec:nested} traces that rise to the number of rows. The interim score, on
the first 349 servers to finish both phases, gave 0.985 and 0.999. At 32 probes 90 of the 100
queries have recall 1.0, nine have 0.9 and one has 0.8. At 128 probes 99 queries have recall 1.0
and one has 0.9, so the mean of 0.999 is one query missing one neighbour. Resampling the 100
queries gives 95 percent bootstrap intervals of 0.982 to 0.995 at 32 probes and 0.997 to 1.000 at
128. These intervals describe variation within this query sample. They say nothing about other
shards' subspaces, which Section~\ref{sec:regen} measures. The earlier scale points recorded only
the mean, so they carry no interval.

The $10^{9}$ row has no routed-versus-scan figure in the table because that run carried a cold
store of the original float vectors and measured true recall instead. The routed shortlist at 128
probes returned 0.999 of the compressed scan there too, the compressed scan itself found only 0.592
of the true neighbours, and reranking the shortlist against the stored originals raised that to
0.991. That row is the reason Section~\ref{sec:limits} exists.

The runs of Table~\ref{tab:scale} differ in more than size. Each was a separate index with its own
bootstrap shard, coarse quantizer and queries, so a change between them could come from any of
those. The trillion-row run removes that confound. The exact top ten over any subset of servers is
the merge of that subset's partials, and so is the routed top ten, so recall against the exact scan
of any subset is exact and needs no new scan. A subset of $k$ servers is a corpus of $2k$ billion
rows from the same generator, with the same basis, coarse quantizer, router and queries, and along
$k$ only the number of rows changes. We took the first $k$ servers and five seeded random subsets
of $k$ servers for twelve values of $k$ from 1 to 500. The random subsets are drawn uniformly
from all servers. Up to $k=20$ none of them holds any of the four servers the queries came from,
servers 0, 125, 250 and 375, and from $k=50$ on they hold up to four. The first $k$ servers always
include server 0.

Table~\ref{tab:nested} and Figure~\ref{fig:nested} give the result. At 128 probes recall moves
from 0.991 at two billion rows to 0.999 at a trillion, and at 256 probes from 0.999 to 1.000. At
narrow widths recall rises with the number of rows, at 16 probes from 0.797 to 0.952 and at 32
probes from 0.905 to 0.989. Between one and ten servers, where no random subset holds a server the
queries came from, recall at 32 probes already rises from 0.905 to 0.943, so the rise does not
depend on those servers. Growing the corpus from two billion to a trillion rows therefore causes
no measurable loss of routed recall at any width under this protocol, and at narrow widths it
raises it. The rise at 32 probes across the separate runs, 0.979, 0.982, 0.983 and 0.989, is the
same effect with the confound present.

Every reference neighbour lives in one of the 2048 cells, and each query orders the cells by the
bound the router uses. Inside a probed cell the routed pass scores every row with the same
asymmetric distance the exact scan used, so a row has the same score in both passes and, unless the
tenth score is tied and the tie broken differently, a reference neighbour is returned exactly when
its cell is probed. Recall at width $p$ is the share of
reference neighbours whose cell has rank below $p$ in its query's order. That identity, stated over
finite sets with exhaustive in-cell scoring as its hypothesis, is checked in Lean 4 with Mathlib in
the repository (\texttt{lean/Pvldb1t/Reachability.lean}, theorem \texttt{recall\_eq\_share}). The
proof establishes the set relationship under its hypotheses and nothing more. It needs the
reference neighbours, so it does not predict which rows become neighbours as the corpus grows, and
it says nothing about other corpora or query populations.

What it gives is an instrument and a check. The share needs the cell of each of the 1000 reference
neighbours and nothing else, so it was computed from the index metadata in 500 jobs of half a
minute each and one merge, with no distance scan. Table~\ref{tab:reach} and
Figure~\ref{fig:reach} give it at nine widths. It agreed with the two widths already measured, 32
and 128, to the last digit. We then recorded the values at 16, 64 and 256, submitted a routed pass
at those widths on all 500 servers, and the measured recall came back 0.952, 0.997 and 1.000, equal
to the recorded values at every width. That agreement validates the implementation of the routed
pass, the merge and the census against one another. The neighbours' cell ranks have median 1,
ninetieth percentile 9, ninety-ninth percentile 32 and maximum 169, so the one neighbour that 128
probes miss sits in the 170th cell of its query's order.

The identity also frames the stability across scale. The corpus grows by adding shards under one
shared coarse quantizer, so an added shard puts rows into the same 2048 cells and leaves every
query's probe order unchanged. Recall at width $p$ changes only if the ten nearest rows move to
cells of different rank. Nothing forces nearer rows into earlier cells, since a row near a cell
boundary can be close to the query and sit in a cell the bound ranks late. Whether they do is an
empirical question. By the identity, recall at width $p$ is the share of reference neighbours in
the first $p$ cells, so the nested series measures that share as rows are added, and on this index
it rises at narrow widths and does not fall at any width. That is an observation about this corpus
and router, not a consequence of the identity, and a corpus whose new rows land near cell
boundaries could behave otherwise.

\subsection{The cells, query placement, and queries outside the index}
\label{sec:follow}

The same 500 metadata jobs took a census of the cells. The 2048 cells hold between 49.2 million
and 2.15 billion rows, with mean 488 million and median 418 million, a Gini coefficient of 0.328,
the largest one percent of cells holding 3.7 percent of rows, and no empty cell.

The 1000 reference neighbours of the corpus-row queries sit on 233 of the 500 servers, and one
server holds 16.6 percent of them, because each shard's own basis keeps a query's neighbours near
its home shard. Losing one server at random would cost 0.002 of recall in expectation, and losing
that one server would cost 0.166. Because every query is a corpus row, it is also its own nearest
neighbour.

The non-member set removes both. Its reference scan, routed passes at 32 and 128 probes and exact
merge were rerun over all 500 servers with the programs of the first run. Its 1000 reference
neighbours sit on 404 of the 500 servers, no server holds more than 1.0 percent of them, and the
median query has its ten neighbours on eight servers. Routed recall against the exact scan was
0.969 at 32 probes and 0.999 at 128, with 95 percent bootstrap intervals of 0.954 to 0.982 and 0.997
to 1.000. At 32 probes 78 of the 100 queries had recall 1.0 and the lowest had 0.6. At 128 probes
the two sets agree. At 32 probes the queries the index does not contain lose 0.020, which we
attribute to the two advantages a corpus row has, its own row among its ten neighbours and
neighbours in the cells its own shard's basis favours. Section~\ref{sec:regen} separates the two.

\subsection{Queries from two hundred shards}
\label{sec:regen}

Both four-shard sets sample four of the generator's subspaces, and a bootstrap over their queries
cannot show how recall varies across subspaces. The many-shard set samples two hundred, one query
per shard, half of them corpus rows from shards on 100 different servers and half from seeds no
corpus shard uses. By the time it was drawn the 500 index volumes had been released. The corpus is
a seed, so we measured it on a regenerated index. Each of 500 jobs regenerates its server's 400
shards one at a time into scratch space with the build's own code and the bootstrap's basis,
assigns the rows to cells with the global coarse quantizer and adopts the bootstrap's radii, runs
the exact scan and the routed search at 32 and 128 probes on that shard, keeps the best ten per
query, and discards the shard. Merging top-ten lists across shards is exact, so the result equals
a search of the whole server. Every regenerated shard file and its two cell files are hashed and
compared with the sha256 the first run recorded for the released index. PENDING-1TRG-RESULTS.

\section{How the measurement ran}
\label{sec:run}

\paragraph{Jobs.} Every job in the build and the measurement requested one CPU and two gigabytes
of memory, the cluster's smallest class, except 164 exact scans submitted in an earlier session
at six CPUs and eight gigabytes. The pool resumed at one CPU, kept those 164 partials and scanned
the other 336 servers. The median scan call took 2573 seconds at six CPUs and 2757 seconds at one,
so six cores bought about seven percent on a scan that holds one. A scan over memory-mapped shards fits two gigabytes once
three choices are made. The queries come from the cache, because deriving them from the seed
regenerates four whole five-million-row blocks. At most two shards are open, because a mapped shard

the seed in row bands and drops each written band from the page cache, because written pages count
against the job's memory limit. Table~\ref{tab:budget} gives what one job of each kind used.

\paragraph{Submission.} The driver publishes each job to a controller \cite{natsbursting} that
creates it only while the namespace has fewer than five jobs pending and twenty running and the
cluster has fewer than a hundred pending pods, and retries a deferred job after waits that double
from thirty seconds to fifteen minutes, for up to fifteen attempts, following an earlier design for
polite submission to shared clusters \cite{politesubmit}. Above the controller a pool keeps at most
twenty jobs in flight with no wave barrier, checkpoints each phase to a state file, adopts jobs
still on the cluster after a restart, recycles a failed job after a back-off from two minutes to
thirty, and waits ninety minutes for a submitted job to appear before it treats the job as lost,
because a job the controller has deferred is not yet visible and resubmitting it would queue a
duplicate.

\paragraph{Safety of repeated jobs.} Every job checks for its output and exits if it is there,
writes its output under a temporary name and renames it into place. A duplicate, a retry and a job adopted after a restart therefore
compute the same partial and either find it done or replace it whole. Two cases are weaker than
that, and we state them. The temporary name was per output and not per attempt, and Kubernetes
allows only one job of a name, but a pod can outlive its job on a node that no longer responds, so
two live writers for one server were not excluded by construction. And the score phase loads every
partial it finds, and the figure script asserts 500 per phase, but nothing checks shapes or
duplicate files. The
regenerated-index run of Section~\ref{sec:regen} writes under names unique to each attempt and
loads every existing output before it treats the output as done.

\paragraph{Failures.} Table~\ref{tab:pool} gives what the pool did. Two failure classes dominated.
One node returned a storage read fault to every job placed on it within 3 to 13 minutes of start,
and retries landed elsewhere. Pods pending past 45 minutes were recycled in the first run, and in
every instance checked the original pod had run and the retry found its output within minutes. In
the second sweep that rule was replaced by waiting, because a running job is never deleted on a
namespace other groups share. Six pods left on nodes that no longer responded were removed by
hand across the four days. Six index volumes became unreachable when their node went offline, and
each was recreated and rebuilt from its seed in 2 hours 32 minutes to 3 hours 49 minutes. No backup
of the index was kept.

It is not a statement about recall against the truth. The compressed scan at $10^{9}$ rows found
0.592 of the float neighbours, and on a real corpus of 15 million Cohere Embed v3 vectors the same
index found 0.693 of them by exact scan, 0.689 by routing at 256 probes, and 0.981 after reranking
the routed shortlist against stored originals. On that corpus the routed shortlist returned 0.990
of the compressed scan at 256 probes, so the smaller real-data pilot supports the routing result,
and the compression result does not carry over. Four bits per dimension at this rate loses
neighbours that only a rerank tier recovers, and the trillion-row corpus, at intrinsic dimension 16
against a real embedding's 52 to 61, cannot show that.

A cold store of originals at $10^{12}$ rows would be 128 TB of float vectors, so true recall is
established at $10^{9}$ and at the real pilot, not here. The seeded corpus does allow one bound.
The float rows named by a shortlist can be regenerated from the seed, so the compressed top-10 of
each query can be reranked by float cosine within the shortlist that contains it, and the share of
the compressed top-10 that survives that rerank is an upper bound on compressed-only true recall,
because a corpus float neighbour that lies in the shortlist is a shortlist float neighbour. The set
inequality behind the bound is checked in Lean 4 with Mathlib
(\texttt{lean/Pvldb1t/RerankBound.lean}, theorem \texttt{rerank\_upper\_bound}), under the same
caveat as the identity, that the proof covers the set relationship and not the data.

routed shortlists behave the same way, 0.623 at 16 probes and 0.621 at 256. After reranking, the
routed top-10 agrees with the reranked reference top-10 at 0.940 for 16 probes, 0.982 for 32, 0.996
for 64 and 1.000 from 128 on, within 0.012 of the recall before reranking at every width. That
agreement is between two reranked shortlists of width 100, each a restricted candidate set, and it
is not float recall over the corpus.

\section{Related work}

Product quantization with asymmetric distance computation and inverted files
\cite{jegou2011product} is the basis of the index, and the two-stage arrangement in which a
compressed scan produces a shortlist that a finer code or the original vectors rerank dates from the
same group's billion-vector work \cite{jegou2011rerank}. Locally optimized product quantization
\cite{kalantidis2014lopq} learns a separate rotation and codebook for each cell, the opposite choice
to the shared basis we use to keep scores comparable across shards, and anisotropic quantization
\cite{guo2020scann} and RaBitQ \cite{gao2024rabitq} are the current forms of the quantizer with, in
the latter case, an error bound on the estimated distance. Locally adaptive scalar quantization
\cite{aguerrebere2023lvq} puts a compressed representation under a graph index and reranks from a
second tier, the arrangement our $10^{9}$ and real-embedding figures use. Locality-sensitive hashing
\cite{indyk1998lsh} and navigable graphs \cite{malkov2020hnsw} are the usual alternatives to the
inverted file and are not what this paper measures.

Billion-scale search on one machine or one node's disk is established
\cite{johnson2019faiss,subramanya2019diskann,chen2021spann}, and the NeurIPS'21 Big ANN competition
\cite{simhadri2022bigann} fixed the datasets and the reporting for that scale, with its 2023
successor \cite{simhadri2024bigann23} turning to filtered, sparse, out-of-distribution and
streaming search at ten million rows. Distributed vector databases such as Milvus
\cite{wang2021milvus} and its cloud-native successor \cite{guo2022manu} shard an index across nodes
and merge per-node results, the arrangement our fleet uses without a serving layer, recent work
places a billion-scale index on distributed storage \cite{yu2025dsann}, and a single graph index
has been spread over more than a thousand machines at $5 \times 10^{10}$ vectors
\cite{adams2025distributedann}.

The closest precedents are at our scale. The Faiss library paper \cite{douze2024faiss} indexes 1.5
trillion 144-dimensional vectors, compressed to 54 bytes each, built as 2000 shards on a few hundred
servers and regrouped into 100 indexes by inverted list, memory-mapped on one machine and searched
through 20 intermediate servers in about a second per query. It reports the construction and the
search time and no recall at that scale. UBASE \cite{tian2026ubase} serves about a trillion vectors
at ByteDance and reports latency and memory for that deployment and recall on sets of up to about a
hundred million vectors. Both are systems that run at a trillion vectors. This paper is a
measurement at a trillion rows. We found no published comparison of a routed index against the
exact scan of the same index at that scale, no scale series inside one index, and no record from
which such numbers can be recomputed.

\section{Conclusion}

A trillion-row compressed index was scanned exactly and searched by routing over the same shards.
Scaling from $10^{8}$ to $10^{12}$ rows causes no measurable loss of routed recall against the exact
scan for corpus-row queries, inside the one index recall at narrow widths rises with the number of
rows, and queries the index does not contain give 0.969 at 32 probes and 0.999 at 128.
PENDING-1TRG-CONCLUSION. Recall at any width is the share of reference neighbours whose cell the
router reaches, a count that matched five measured widths, and the work that buys it is a fixed
share of the index per query, a median of 19 billion rows at 32 probes. Against float rows
regenerated from the seed, the compressed ranking keeps at most 0.621 of the true neighbours. All of
it ran in a shared cluster's smallest job class and can be recomputed from the archived record.
