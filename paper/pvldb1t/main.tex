\begin{abstract}
We report a measurement of routed inverted-file search over an index of one trillion 4-bit
quantized vectors, built and queried on the National Research Platform's shared Kubernetes
cluster. The index is 500 servers of two billion rows each, 24.0 bytes per row, 24 TB in all, on
one block volume per server. Against the exact full scan of the same compressed index, routed
search returns recall at ten of 0.989 when it probes 32 of 2048 cells and 0.999 when it probes
128, on 100 queries, with 95 percent bootstrap intervals of 0.982 to 0.995 and 0.997 to 1.000. The
same protocol gave 0.979 and 0.999 at one hundred million rows, 0.982 and 0.999 at ten billion,
and 0.983 and 0.999 at one hundred billion. Recall against the full scan does not degrade across
four orders of magnitude of corpus size. Inside the trillion-row index, where subsets of servers
differ only in the number of rows, recall is flat at 128 probes and rises with the number of rows
at 16 and 32 probes. Every scale point draws its queries by the same recipe, the
first rows of four seeded shards spread across the index, so the comparison across sizes holds the
query distribution fixed. That recipe also places the reference neighbours on few servers, and a
query set drawn from many shards is not measured here. The second result is that this
recall is a property of the router alone. From the cell of each reference neighbour in its
query's probe order we predicted recall at nine probe widths without a scan, the prediction
matched the two widths already measured to the last digit, and a sweep at three further widths,
run after the prediction was recorded, returned 0.952, 0.997 and 1.000, each equal to its
predicted value. The measure is self-consistency of the compressed index on a synthetic corpus
of intrinsic dimension 16, and the paper gives the float ground truth and real-embedding figures
that bound it. Reranking each shortlist against float rows regenerated from the seed shows that the
compressed ranking keeps at most 0.621 of the true neighbours on this corpus, and after that rerank the routed
top ten agrees with the reranked reference at 0.982 for 32 probes and 1.000 from 128 on. The build and the job design that
made the measurement possible in the cluster's smallest job class are described in a companion
paper. Every log, state file,
partial result and score is in the repository, and the two identities the analysis rests on are
checked in Lean 4 with Mathlib.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Approximate nearest-neighbour search over compressed vectors is routine at a billion rows
\cite{jegou2011product,johnson2019faiss,subramanya2019diskann,chen2021spann}. The question this
paper answers is narrower than most. When an inverted-file index is partitioned into shards that
share one coarse quantizer and one rotation, and the corpus grows by adding shards rather than by
densifying old ones, does the routing lose anything it would have found by scanning everything?
The way to ask it is to build the whole index, scan the whole index exactly, and compare. We did
that at one trillion rows.

The index was built and measured on the National Research Platform's Nautilus cluster
\cite{weitzel2025nrp}, a Kubernetes federation shared by many research groups across sites. A
fleet measurement there is a matter of fair use by design. Jobs are small, independent and
resumable, submissions are paced by a controller that yields when the cluster is busy, and nothing
is reserved. That design, the seeded corpus that made the build move no data, and the pool
that ran the measurement are the subject of a companion paper \cite{bond2026fleet}.

The paper makes two claims and states one limit. Routed recall against the exact scan of the
same index is 0.989 at 32 probes and 0.999 at 128 probes at $10^{12}$ rows, with 95 percent
bootstrap intervals over the 100 queries of 0.982 to 0.995 and 0.997 to 1.000, and the same
protocol at $10^{8}$, $10^{10}$ and $10^{11}$ rows gave figures within 0.01 of these at 32 probes
and within 0.001 at 128 probes (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Inside the trillion-row index, where
subsets of servers differ only in the number of rows, recall is flat at 128 probes and rises with
the number of rows at 16 and 32 probes (Section~\ref{sec:nested}). The queries at every scale point are
the first rows of four seeded shards spread across the index, so the trend across sizes is
measured with the query recipe fixed, and Section~\ref{sec:follow} states what that recipe does
to the placement of the reference neighbours. That recall is the share of reference
neighbours whose cell the router reaches, which was computed from the index metadata alone,
matched the measured widths, and predicted three further widths before they were measured
(Section~\ref{sec:reach}). The limit is that the corpus is synthetic, of intrinsic dimension about 16,
and the recall reported is against the compressed index's own exact scan rather than against
float ground truth. Section~\ref{sec:limits} gives the figures that bound the result, including a
real-embedding pilot on which the routing holds and the compression alone does not.

\section{The index and the corpus}
\label{sec:system}


\paragraph{Index.} Each row is a 32-dimensional vector projected to 24 dimensions by one basis
shared by the whole fleet, quantized to 4 bits per dimension, and assigned to one of 2048
inverted-file cells by one shared coarse quantizer. Both the basis and the quantizer are fitted
once, on a bootstrap shard, and read by every build job. A shard holds five million rows. A
server holds 400 shards, two billion rows, on one block volume. The on-disk row is 12 bytes of
codes, 8 bytes of per-row norms used by the asymmetric distance computation, and 4 bytes of cell
membership. Every server reports 48\,009\,656\,624 index bytes for its two billion rows, 24.0
bytes per row, and the fleet holds 24.0 TB of index on 500 volumes. Scoring is asymmetric
distance computation over the codes, in row blocks, from memory-mapped shards. Because the basis
and the coarse quantizer are shared, scores from different shards are comparable and a global
top-$k$ is the exact merge of per-shard top-$k$s. That property is what makes the measurement
below exact rather than approximate, and the companion paper \cite{bond2026fleet} draws the
arrangement.

\paragraph{Corpus.} The corpus is defined by a seed and regenerated on demand, so the distributed
build moves no corpus bytes. Each global shard draws a fresh rank-16 Gaussian basis, projects
Gaussian coefficients through it with a linear decay of singular values from 1.0 to 0.3, and adds
five percent noise, in an ambient dimension of 32. Its intrinsic dimension measures 15.4 to 17.0
at every size we tested, with no trend in row count. Real embeddings such as Cohere Embed v3
at 1024 dimensions measure 52 to 61 by the same estimator. Hubness, the concentration of
nearest-neighbour lists on a few points \cite{radovanovic2010hubs}, rises with corpus size on
this recipe, at about 0.42 per decade at $k=10$ against roughly 0.25 per decade on real data. We
attribute the rise to how shards are added. Each shard draws its own random 16-dimensional subspace, and two
random 16-dimensional subspaces of a 32-dimensional space are not orthogonal, so the rows of a new
shard land close to rows of shards already present. Every added shard therefore adds rows near
existing ones, and the rows that sit where many subspaces pass close together appear in more and
more neighbour lists. Whatever its cause, the rise does not act on the quantity measured here. Recall against the exact scan depends only on whether
the router reaches the cell that holds each reference neighbour (Section~\ref{sec:reach}), and
hubness changes which rows are the neighbours without changing that condition.

\paragraph{Build.} The 500 servers were built between 2026-08-04 and 2026-09-24 by one job
per server, each computing its 400 shards from the seed, so the index can be rebuilt and checked
from the seeds alone. The build, its storage and its job shape are described in the companion
paper \cite{bond2026fleet}.

\paragraph{Scale points.} The same protocol has been run at $10^{8}$ rows on 4 servers,
$10^{9}$ on 4, $10^{10}$ on 8, $10^{11}$ on 50 and now $10^{12}$ on 500. Per-server work is
constant from $10^{11}$ on, two billion rows, so the step from 50 to 500 servers changes nothing
per node and changes only the number of chances to draw a slow or failing one.

\section{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The measurement has four phases, each a pool of independent jobs on the cluster's batch system,
every submission through a controller that paces submissions, caps concurrency at twenty and
yields while the cluster is busy. The query-cache phase is one job that derives the query set,
the first 25 rows of each of four seeded shards, 100 queries, and writes it to the shared volume.
The queries are corpus rows by construction, so every server sees the same queries without
regenerating anything. The reference phase is one job per server that scans that server's two
billion rows exactly with the asymmetric distance computation and writes its top-10 per query
with scores. The routed phase is one job per server that runs the inverted-file search at 32 and
at 128 probes and writes the same shape of partial. The score phase is one job that merges the
500 reference partials into the exact global top-10, merges the routed partials the same way,
and reports recall at ten of the routed set against the reference set, per probe width, averaged
over queries. A second routed phase at 16, 64 and 256 probes, run two days later, and three
metadata phases that read only the partials and the index headers are described with their
results in Sections~\ref{sec:reach} and~\ref{sec:follow}.

Every job is idempotent on the volume. A job whose output already exists prints that fact and
exits, and outputs are written to a temporary name and renamed, so a job interrupted mid-write leaves
nothing behind. This is the property every recovery rule of the pool relies on \cite{bond2026fleet}.
The score program has not changed since the $10^{9}$ run. The two phase scripts changed at this
run to fit the smallest job class \cite{bond2026fleet}, and none of the changes touch what is computed.

\subsection{Recall across four orders of magnitude}

Table~\ref{tab:scale} and Figure~\ref{fig:scale} give the number the run exists to produce. At
$10^{12}$ rows, routed search at 128 probes of 2048 cells returns 0.999 of the exact scan's top
ten, and at 32 probes 0.989. At 128 probes the figure is 0.999, 0.9988 and 0.9986 at the three
smaller scales, a spread of 0.0004. At 32 probes it rises slowly with scale, 0.979, 0.982, 0.983,
0.989, and Section~\ref{sec:nested} traces that rise to the number of rows alone. The interim score, taken on the first 349 servers to finish
both phases, gave 0.985 and 0.999, and recall at 128 probes on the first half of those 349
servers alone was also 0.999. The number is stable inside the run as well as across runs.

The mean hides little. At 32 probes 90 of the 100 queries have recall 1.0, nine have 0.9 and
one has 0.8. At 128 probes 99 queries have recall 1.0 and one has 0.9, so the mean of 0.999 is one
query missing one neighbour. Resampling the 100 queries gives 95 percent bootstrap intervals of
0.982 to 0.995 at 32 probes and 0.997 to 1.000 at 128. The earlier scale points recorded only the
mean, so they carry no interval.

The $10^{9}$ row has no routed-versus-scan figure in the table because that run's purpose was
different. It carried a cold store of the original float vectors, so it measured true recall
against float ground truth. The routed shortlist at 128 probes returned 0.999 of the compressed
scan there too, the compressed scan itself found only 0.592 of the true neighbours, and reranking
the shortlist against the stored originals raised that to 0.991. That row is the reason
Section~\ref{sec:limits} exists.

The four runs of Table~\ref{tab:scale} differ in more than size. Each was a separate index with
its own bootstrap shard, coarse quantizer and queries, so a change between them could come from
any of those. The trillion-row run removes that confound. The exact top ten over any subset of
servers is the merge of that subset's partials, and so is the routed top ten, so recall against
the exact scan of any subset is exact and needs no new scan. A subset of $k$ servers is a corpus
of $2k$ billion rows from the same generator, with the same basis, coarse quantizer, router and
queries, and along $k$ only the number of rows changes. We took the first $k$ servers and five
seeded random subsets of $k$ servers for twelve values of $k$ from 1 to 500. The random subsets
hold at most one of the four servers the queries came from.

Table~\ref{tab:nested} and Figure~\ref{fig:nested} give the result. At 128 probes recall moves
from 0.991 at two billion rows to 0.999 at a trillion, and at 256 probes from 0.999 to 1.000. At
narrow widths recall rises with the number of rows, at 16 probes from 0.797 to 0.952 and at 32
probes from 0.905 to 0.989. The first $k$ servers give the same curve within the random subsets'
spread, so the rise does not come from the servers that hold the queries' home shards. Growing
the corpus from two billion to a trillion rows therefore causes no measurable loss of routed
recall at any width under this protocol, and at narrow widths it raises it. The rise at 32 probes
across the separate runs, 0.979, 0.982, 0.983 and 0.989, is the same effect with the confound
present.

Every reference neighbour lives in one of the 2048 cells, and each query orders the cells by
the bound the router uses. Recall at width $p$ is then the share of reference neighbours whose
cell has rank below $p$ in its query's order, because inside a probed cell the routed pass scores
with the same asymmetric distance the exact scan used, so a reference neighbour is returned
exactly when its cell was probed. That identity, stated over finite sets with the in-cell
condition as its hypothesis, is checked in Lean 4 with Mathlib in the repository
(\texttt{lean/Pvldb1t/Reachability.lean}, theorem \texttt{recall\_eq\_share}). The share needs
the cell of each of the 1000 reference neighbours and nothing else, so it was computed from the
index metadata in 500 jobs of half a minute each and one merge, with no distance scan.
Table~\ref{tab:reach} and Figure~\ref{fig:reach} give it at nine widths. It agreed with the two
widths already measured, 32 and 128, to the last digit. We then recorded the values at 16, 64
and 256, submitted a routed pass at those three widths on all 500 servers, and the measured
recall came back 0.952, 0.997 and 1.000, again equal to the prediction at every width. The
neighbours' cell ranks have median 1, ninetieth percentile 9, ninety-ninth percentile 32 and
maximum 169, so the single neighbour that 128 probes miss sits in the 170th cell of its query's
order, and 256 probes return every neighbour of every query.

The identity also explains the stability across scale. The corpus grows by adding shards under
one shared coarse quantizer, so an added shard puts rows into the same 2048 cells and leaves every
query's probe order unchanged. What changes is which rows are the ten nearest, and recall at width
$p$ changes only if those rows move to cells of different rank. More rows in every cell put the
nearest ten closer to the query, and nearer rows sit in cells earlier in its probe order, which is
the rise at narrow widths that Section~\ref{sec:nested} measures. The agreement says that
on this index recall is a property of the coarse quantizer's probe order and of nothing downstream
of it. It also gives a cheap instrument. Once the reference neighbours
are known, the recall curve at any width is a count over cell ranks, and a routed pass at a new
width confirms a number that was already on the page.

\subsection{What the index metadata says}
\label{sec:follow}

The same 500 metadata jobs took a census of the cells. The 2048 cells hold between 49.2 million
and 2.15 billion rows, with mean 488 million and median 418 million, a Gini coefficient of
0.328, the largest one percent of cells holding 3.7 percent of rows, and no empty cell. The
imbalance is the shared coarse quantizer's, fitted once on the bootstrap shard, and it is what
the router's probe order works against.

The 1000 reference neighbours of the 100 queries sit on 233 of the 500 servers, and one server
holds 16.6 percent of them, because the queries are rows of four seeded shards and each shard's
own basis keeps a query's neighbours near its home shard. Losing one server at random would cost
0.002 of recall in expectation, and losing that one server would cost 0.166. The same recipe is
used at every scale point, the first rows of four shards spread evenly across the index's shard
range, 25 rows each at $10^{8}$ and $10^{12}$ rows and 125 each at $10^{10}$ and $10^{11}$. The
trend across sizes is therefore measured with the query distribution fixed. What the recipe does
not show is the absolute recall for queries spread over many shards, whose neighbours would fall
on more servers and into cells of other ranks. Measuring it takes a query set drawn from many
shards and a new exact scan. The scan's cost is set by the number of queries and not by how many
shards they come from, so 100 queries taken two from each of 50 shards would cost the same 418
CPU-hours as this run, and 500 queries about five times as much.

\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:cost}

The exact scan of one server's two billion rows at one CPU took 44 minutes at the median server,
and a routed pass 15 to 27 minutes depending on width. Those times include pulling the image,
installing dependencies, cloning the repository and opening 400 shards from network block
storage, so they are the cost of the measurement job and not of inverted-file search, and the
ratio between them is not a latency figure for the method. The first run, the exact scan and
the routed passes at 32 and 128 probes, consumed 772 CPU-hours, and the second sweep at 16, 64
and 256 probes consumed 577, 1349 CPU-hours in all. The per-phase distribution of wall time
and the behaviour of the pool are in the companion paper \cite{bond2026fleet}.

\section{What the result does and does not show}
\label{sec:limits}

Recall of the routed shortlist against the exact scan of the same compressed index does not
degrade from $10^{8}$ to $10^{12}$ rows. That is a statement about the inverted-file layer, its
shared coarse quantizer and its merge. Growing the corpus by adding shards causes no measurable
loss of routed recall against the exact scan at any width measured, at narrow widths it raises

It is not a statement about recall against the truth. The compressed scan at $10^{9}$ rows found
0.592 of the float neighbours, and on a real corpus of 15 million Cohere Embed v3 vectors the same
index found 0.693 of them by exact scan, 0.689 by routing at 256 probes, and 0.981 after reranking
the routed shortlist against stored originals. On that real corpus the routed shortlist returned
0.990 of the compressed scan at 256 probes, so the routing result transfers, and the compression
result does not. Four bits per dimension at this rate loses neighbours that only a rerank tier
recovers, and the trillion-row corpus, at intrinsic dimension 16 against a real embedding's 52 to
61, cannot show that. It is an easy corpus for the code and a hard one only for the hubness the
shards' overlap induces.

A cold store of originals at $10^{12}$ rows would be 128 TB of float vectors, so true recall and
reranking remain established at $10^{9}$ and at the 15-million-row real pilot, not here. The
seeded corpus does allow one bound. The float rows named by a shortlist can be regenerated from
the seed, so the ADC top-10 of each query can be reranked by float cosine within the shortlist
that contains it, and the share of the ADC top-10 that survives that rerank is an upper bound on
ADC-only true recall, because a corpus float neighbour that lies in the shortlist is a shortlist
float neighbour. The set inequality behind the bound is checked in Lean 4 with Mathlib in the
repository (\texttt{lean/Pvldb1t/RerankBound.lean}, theorem \texttt{rerank\_upper\_bound}).
Table~\ref{tab:rerank} gives the result on 11002 regenerated rows. Of the reference scan's ADC top-10, 0.621 survives the float rerank of its own shortlist on average, with 22 of 100 queries keeping all ten and a minimum of 0.100, so ADC-only true recall at $10^{12}$ rows is at most 0.621 on this corpus. The routed shortlists behave the same way, 0.623 at 16 probes and 0.621 at 256. At one billion rows the cold store gave ADC-only true recall of 0.592, inside this bound. The mean cosine of the ADC top-10 to the query is 0.863 and of the reranked top-10 0.929. Each query is itself a corpus row, and that row is in the ADC top-10 for 100 of 100 queries and in the reranked top-10 for 100 of 100.

\section{Related work}

Product quantization with asymmetric distance computation and inverted files
\cite{jegou2011product} is the basis of the index, and the two-stage arrangement in which a
compressed scan produces a shortlist that a finer code or the original vectors rerank dates from
the same group's billion-vector work \cite{jegou2011rerank}. Locally optimized codebooks per
cell \cite{kalantidis2014lopq} are the nearest relative of the shared-basis arrangement we use to
keep scores comparable across shards, and anisotropic quantization \cite{guo2020scann} and RaBitQ
\cite{gao2024rabitq} are the current forms of the quantizer with, in the latter case, an error
bound on the estimated distance. Locally adaptive scalar quantization \cite{aguerrebere2023lvq}
puts a compressed representation under a graph index and reranks from a second tier, which is
the rerank tier our $10^{9}$ and real-embedding figures use. Locality-sensitive hashing
\cite{indyk1998lsh} and navigable graphs \cite{malkov2020hnsw} are the usual alternatives to the
inverted file and are not what this paper measures.

Billion-scale practice is established. Faiss \cite{johnson2019faiss,douze2024faiss} on one
machine or one GPU, DiskANN \cite{subramanya2019diskann} and SPANN \cite{chen2021spann} on a
single node with the index on disk, and the two Big ANN competitions
\cite{simhadri2022bigann,simhadri2024bigann23} that fixed the datasets and the reporting for that
scale. Distributed vector databases such as Milvus \cite{wang2021milvus} and its cloud-native
successor \cite{guo2022manu} shard an index across nodes and merge per-node results, which is
the arrangement our fleet uses without a serving layer, and recent work places a billion-scale
index on distributed storage \cite{yu2025dsann}. The literature the authors searched stops at
$10^{9}$ to $10^{10}$ rows for measured recall against an exact reference, and we found no
published measurement of a routed index against the exact scan of the same index at $10^{12}$
rows. This paper adds that measurement and the identity that predicts it from the router's probe
order. The companion paper \cite{bond2026fleet} describes how it was obtained on a shared cluster.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

The repository at the availability URL carries the corpus seeds and generator, the build,
scan, routed, score and metadata scripts, the pool driver, and under
\texttt{benchmarks/fleet/record/1t/} the pool state files, every driver log, the pilot logs, the
interim score and the two score logs whose RESULT\_JSON lines hold every per-server wall time.
The figures and the cost table are produced from those logs by \texttt{paper/pvldb1t/figdata/}
\texttt{fig\_data.py}. The Lean development under \texttt{paper/pvldb1t/lean/} builds against
Mathlib at Lean 4.32.2, and its axiom audit, recorded in \texttt{AXIOMS.txt}, shows every
theorem depending on propositional extensionality, quotient soundness and choice only. The
twelve repository commits that job containers cloned during the first run are listed in the
result note, and the configuration map that carried the phase scripts is recorded by content
hash. Rebuilding the index from seed costs about three and a half hours per server at the pool's
width.

\section{Conclusion}

A trillion-row compressed index was scanned exactly and searched by routing over the same
shards. Scaling from $10^{8}$ to $10^{12}$ rows causes no measurable loss of routed recall
against the exact scan under this protocol, inside the one index recall at narrow widths rises
with the number of rows, and the recall at any probe width is the share of reference neighbours whose cell the
router reaches, a count that predicted three measured widths to the last digit. Against float
rows regenerated from the seed, the compressed ranking keeps at most 0.621 of the true neighbours,
and a rerank of the routed shortlist recovers the reranked reference at 0.982 for 32 probes and
1.000 from 128 on. The measurement
cost 1349 CPU-hours and four days, and it is reproducible from the repository, which carries the
seeds, the scripts, the driver, the state files, the driver logs, the pilots, the interim score
and the final scores with every per-server wall time.
