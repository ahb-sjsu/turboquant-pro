\begin{abstract}
We measure routed inverted-file search over one trillion 4-bit quantized vectors against the exact
scan of the same compressed index. The index is 500 servers of two billion rows, 24 TB, built from
a seeded synthetic corpus on the National Research Platform's shared Kubernetes cluster. Probing 32
of 2048 cells, routed search returns 0.989 of the compressed index's exact top ten for queries that are corpus rows
and 0.969 for queries the index does not contain, and probing 128 cells it returns 0.999 for both.
Two hundred further queries, one from each of 200 shards, measured on a regenerated
998-billion-row subset of the index, the shards a rebuild from the seed reproduced, give 0.982 and
0.977 for the two kinds at 32 probes and 0.999 and 1.000 at 128. Within the trillion-row index, subsets of servers that differ only in their
number of rows show no loss of recall as rows are added, for both query sets, and a gain at narrow
widths, the behaviour the Faiss authors describe for a fixed coarse quantizer, here measured at a
trillion rows. A prediction from twenty servers, registered before the non-member run was scored,
matched its trillion-row recall within error, and a second, from separate corpora of $10^{8}$ to
$10^{9}$ rows, failed at 32 probes. Because a probed cell is scored as the scan scores it, recall at any width is the
share of reference neighbours in the probed cells, and that share, computed from cell metadata,
matched every measured width, three of them measured after it was recorded. The same identity
compares cell orders at equal work, and plain centroid order does as well as the router's
radius-adjusted order, so the result is a property of the ordinary inverted file. Holding recall at
32 probes costs a median of 19 billion rows scored per query, 1.9 percent of the index, on all 500
servers. Against float rows
regenerated from the seed the compressed ranking keeps at most 0.621 of the true neighbours, so the
result is about routing and not about compression. The corpus has intrinsic dimension about 16,
against 52 to 61 for real embeddings, and the only real-data check is one corpus of 15 million
rows, so the trillion-row result is a result on this generator. We describe the jobs, the pool and the
accounting of the run, and archive the record that the tables and figures are computed from.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Approximate nearest-neighbour search over compressed vectors is routine at a billion rows
\cite{jegou2011product,johnson2019faiss,subramanya2019diskann,chen2021spann}, and indexes of a
trillion vectors have been built. The Faiss library paper \cite{douze2024faiss} describes an
index of 1.5 trillion vectors built in 2000 shards on a few hundred servers and searched in about a
second per query, and UBASE \cite{tian2026ubase} serves a production deployment of about a trillion
vectors. Neither reports recall at that scale. UBASE reports recall on sets of up to about a hundred
million vectors and latency and memory at a trillion, and the Faiss account reports the build and
the search time. What is missing at that scale is the comparison that defines the error of routing,
the routed result against an exact scan of the whole index.

What to expect has a precedent. The Faiss authors observe that with a fixed coarse quantizer a
growing dataset can only bring a query's nearest neighbour closer, that a closer neighbour is more
likely to sit in a cell near the query, and that the number of probes needed for a target accuracy
then falls with the dataset size \cite[Section~5.1]{douze2024faiss}. They report it in the regime
where the number of cells is larger than optimal for the dataset, with $2^{18}$ cells for five
million vectors as their example, and measure it as search time at fixed accuracy. This paper does
not offer that mechanism as new. It measures the same direction far on the other side of that
regime, 2048 cells for $10^{12}$ rows, nearly half a billion rows a cell, against an exact scan,
inside one index where nothing but the number of rows changes, and leaves a record from which the
results can be recomputed. Those three things, the trillion-row exact comparison, the controlled
scale series and the record, are the contribution.

This paper makes the comparison at $10^{12}$ rows. When an inverted-file index is partitioned
into shards that share one coarse quantizer and one rotation, and the corpus grows by adding
shards, does the routing lose anything it would have found by scanning everything? We built the
whole index, scanned all of it exactly, ran the routed search over the same shards at five probe
widths, and compared. Three things make the comparison more than one number. The exact scan of any
subset of servers is the merge of that subset's partial results, so one index gives a scale series
in which only the number of rows changes. Recall at any width follows from where the reference
neighbours sit in the router's probe order, so the curve over widths can be computed from metadata
and checked against routed passes. And the corpus is a seed, so every row can be regenerated and
the results recomputed from the record.

The findings are these. Routed recall against the exact scan of the compressed index is 0.989 at 32 probes and 0.999 at
128 probes at $10^{12}$ rows for queries that are corpus rows, with 95 percent bootstrap intervals
over the 100 queries of 0.982 to 0.995 and 0.997 to 1.000, and 0.969 and 0.999 for 100 queries the
index does not contain (Section~\ref{sec:results}). A third set of 200 queries, one from each of
200 shards and measured on the regenerated 998-billion-row subset of the index, gives 0.982 at 32
probes and 0.999 at
128 for its corpus rows and 0.977 and 1.000 for the rest, and at 32 probes each four-shard value
lies inside the new set's 95 percent interval for the same kind of query (Section~\ref{sec:regen}). Inside the trillion-row
index recall moves by less than 0.01 at 128 probes as servers are added and rises at 16 and 32
probes, and for the non-member queries, where no subset changes whether a query is its own
neighbour, it rises at both widths measured (Section~\ref{sec:nested}). The recall curve over nine
widths computed from the cell ranks of the reference neighbours matched the five widths measured,
three of them measured after the curve was recorded (Section~\ref{sec:reach}). At equal rows
scored, plain centroid order gives the same recall as the router's radius-adjusted order or better,
so nothing here depends on that adjustment (Section~\ref{sec:order}). Holding that recall costs a
fixed share of the index per query, a median of 19 billion rows at 32 probes
(Section~\ref{sec:work}). For an index designer the lesson is in two parts. A probe width tuned on a
small slice of a corpus that grows under a fixed coarse quantizer did not have to be widened as the
corpus grew five-hundredfold inside one index, and the work that width buys grows with the corpus,
so keeping work per query bounded would take more cells, not a narrower width, which this paper
does not test. Whether the trillion-row figure could be known in advance we tested by registration:
a deficit law fitted on twenty servers predicted the non-member recall at $10^{12}$ rows within
its error at both widths, and a model chosen on separate corpora of $10^{8}$ to $10^{9}$ rows failed
at 32 probes (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). The limit is that the
corpus is synthetic, of intrinsic dimension about 16, and the reference is the compressed index's
own exact scan. Section~\ref{sec:limits} gives the figures that bound the result, an upper bound of
0.621 on the compressed ranking's true recall at $10^{12}$ rows and a real-embedding pilot.

The index was built and measured on the National Research Platform's Nautilus cluster
\cite{weitzel2025nrp}, which many research groups share and on which nothing is reserved. Every
build job and almost every measurement job ran in the cluster's smallest job class, one CPU and two
gigabytes. Section~\ref{sec:run} describes how, and Section~\ref{sec:cost} accounts for what the run
used.

\paragraph{Index.} Each row is a 32-dimensional vector projected to 24 dimensions by one basis
shared by the whole fleet, quantized to 4 bits per dimension, and assigned to one of 2048
inverted-file cells by one shared coarse quantizer. Both the basis and the quantizer are fitted
once, on a bootstrap shard, and read by every build job. A shard holds five million rows. A server
holds 400 shards, two billion rows, on one block volume. The on-disk row is 12 bytes of codes, 8
bytes of per-row norms used by the asymmetric distance computation, and 4 bytes of cell membership.
Every server reports 48\,009\,656\,624 index bytes for its two billion rows, 24.0 bytes per row,
so the fleet holds 24.0 TB of index, on volumes provisioned at 56 GiB each, 30.1 TB in all.
Scoring is asymmetric distance computation over the codes, in row blocks, from memory-mapped
shards. Because the basis and the coarse quantizer are shared, scores from different shards are
comparable and a global top-$k$ is the exact merge of per-shard top-$k$s (Figure~\ref{fig:fleet}).
That property is what makes the measurement below exact rather than approximate.

\paragraph{Routing.} A query is rotated by the shared basis and compared with the 2048 cell
centroids. A cell's angular radius $r$ is the largest angle between its centroid and the quantized
direction of a bootstrap row assigned to it. Each cell gets the priority $\cos(\max(0, \theta -
r/2))$, $\theta$ the angle from the query to the centroid, and the routed search scores every row of
the $p$ cells of highest priority, on every server, with the same asymmetric distance the exact scan
uses. The radii are the bootstrap's, so every server probes the same cells for a query. The
priority is an ordering heuristic and not a bound. With the full radius it would bound the cosine
of the bootstrap's own rows by the triangle inequality on the sphere, but half the radius does not,
the radius fitted on one shard need not cover the rows of later shards, and the asymmetric score is
not that cosine. Nothing below depends on it being a bound. Section~\ref{sec:order} compares it
with plain centroid order and the full radius at equal work.

\paragraph{Corpus.} The corpus is defined by a seed and regenerated on demand. Each global shard
draws a fresh rank-16 Gaussian basis, projects Gaussian coefficients through it with a linear decay
of singular values from 1.0 to 0.3, and adds five percent noise, in an ambient dimension of 32. A
shard is a pure function of its global index, so any job can produce any shard without reading
anything, and the build moved no corpus bytes. A build from files would first have placed 128 TB of
float vectors where 500 jobs could read them. A single shard is rank 16 plus noise by
construction. Across shards the intrinsic dimension measures 15.4 to 17.0 by the two-nearest-
neighbour estimator \cite{facco2017twonn} on unit-normalized samples of 25000 to 200000 rows drawn
across shards, with no trend whether the rows grow within a fixed set of shards or as 5 to 40 new
shards. These are mixed-shard estimates, and no within-shard estimate was made. Real embeddings such as Cohere Embed v3 at
1024 dimensions measure 52 to 61 by the same estimator. Hubness, the concentration of
nearest-neighbour lists on a few points \cite{radovanovic2010hubs}, rises with corpus size on this
recipe, at about 0.42 per decade at $k=10$ against roughly 0.25 per decade on real data. We
attribute the rise to how shards are added. Each shard draws its own random 16-dimensional subspace,
two random 16-dimensional subspaces of a 32-dimensional space are not orthogonal, and so the rows of
a new shard land close to rows of shards already present. Hubness can act on the result. Recall
depends on whether the router reaches the cells of the reference neighbours
(Section~\ref{sec:reach}), and hubness changes which rows become those neighbours and so which cells
they sit in. The identity of Section~\ref{sec:reach} does not separate that effect, and we did not
measure it. A corpus with the hubness of real embeddings could place its neighbours differently.

\paragraph{Scale points.} The same protocol has been run at $10^{8}$ rows on 4 servers, $10^{9}$
on 4, $10^{10}$ on 8, $10^{11}$ on 50 and $10^{12}$ on 500. Per-server work is constant from
$10^{11}$ on, two billion rows, so the step from 50 to 500 servers changes nothing per node and
changes only the number of chances to draw a slow or failing one.

A measurement is a set of phases, each a pool of independent jobs (Table~\ref{tab:phases}). The
query-cache phase writes the query set to the shared volume once, and every server reads it from
that file. The reference phase is one job per server that scans the server's two billion rows
exactly and writes its top-10 per query with scores. The routed phase is one job per server that
runs the routed search at 32 and 128 probes and writes the same shape of partial. The score phase
merges the 500 reference partials into the exact global top-10, merges the routed partials the
same way, and reports recall at ten of the routed set against the reference set, averaged over
queries. The reference is the exact scan of the compressed index, not float ground truth, so this
recall measures what routing loses and not what compression loses. The second loss is bounded
separately. Against float rows regenerated from the seed, the compressed ranking keeps at most
0.621 of the true neighbours at $10^{12}$ rows (Table~\ref{tab:rerank}), and at $10^{9}$ rows,
where a cold store of float rows was kept, the compressed scan found 0.592 of them. A second routed sweep at 16, 64 and 256 probes was submitted the day the first score
landed and scored two days later, and two metadata phases read only the partials and the cell
metadata (Section~\ref{sec:reach}). Recall is computed by the same function at every scale
point. At $10^{9}$ the run's coordinator computed it. From $10^{10}$ on a separate score program
computes it, and that program has since changed only in its file names and probe widths.

Three query sets were measured. The corpus-row set is the first 25 rows of each of four seeded
shards, 0, 50000, 100000 and 150000, spread evenly across the index, and the same recipe gave the
queries at every earlier scale point, so the comparison across sizes holds the query recipe fixed.
The non-member set is the first 25 rows of each of shards 200000, 250000, 300000 and 350000 of the
same generator, seeds no corpus shard uses, so no query is in the index. Both draw from four
shards, and each shard has its own subspace, so both cover four subspaces of the generator. The
many-shard set covers two hundred. It is row 0 of each of shards 400000 to 400099, none in the
index, and row 0 of one shard on each of 100 servers spread across the index, all in it. It was
measured after the index volumes were released, on the regenerated 998-billion-row subset of the
index, the 199600 shards a rebuild from the seed reproduced (Section~\ref{sec:regen}).

\subsection{Recall across four orders of magnitude}

Table~\ref{tab:scale} and Figure~\ref{fig:scale} give the number the run exists to produce. At
$10^{12}$ rows, routed search at 128 probes of 2048 cells returns 0.999 of the exact scan's top
ten, and at 32 probes 0.989. At 128 probes the figure is 0.999, 0.999, 0.9988 and 0.9986 at the
four smaller scales, a spread of 0.0004. At 32 probes it is 0.979, 0.978, 0.982, 0.983 and 0.989
from $10^{8}$ to $10^{12}$. The step from $10^{8}$ to $10^{9}$ is one neighbour in a thousand, and
the overall rise is traced to the number of rows in Section~\ref{sec:nested}. The interim score, on
the first 349 servers to finish both phases, gave 0.985 and 0.999. At 32 probes 90 of the 100
queries have recall 1.0, nine have 0.9 and one has 0.8. At 128 probes 99 queries have recall 1.0
and one has 0.9, so the mean of 0.999 is one query missing one neighbour. Resampling the 100
queries gives 95 percent bootstrap intervals of 0.982 to 0.995 at 32 probes and 0.997 to 1.000 at
128. These intervals describe variation within this query sample. They say nothing about other
shards' subspaces, which Section~\ref{sec:regen} measures. The earlier scale points recorded only
the mean, so they carry no interval.

The $10^{9}$ run is final, and it differs from the others in two ways. Its four servers answered
queries through a serving path, a coordinator sending each query to warm server processes, where
every later run scanned in batch jobs. The coordinator merged the servers' answers exactly and
computed recall with the function the later score jobs use. And it
carried a cold store of the original float vectors, so it also measured true recall. The compressed
scan found only 0.592 of the true neighbours, and reranking the routed shortlist against the stored
originals raised that to 0.991. That row is the reason Section~\ref{sec:limits} exists.

The runs of Table~\ref{tab:scale} differ in more than size. Each was a separate index with its own
bootstrap shard, coarse quantizer and queries, so a change between them could come from any of
those. The trillion-row run removes that confound. The exact top ten over any subset of servers is
the merge of that subset's partials, and so is the routed top ten, so recall against the exact scan
of any subset is exact and needs no new scan. A subset of $k$ servers is a corpus of $2k$ billion
rows from the same generator, with the same basis, coarse quantizer, router and queries, and along
$k$ only the number of rows changes. We took the first $k$ servers and five seeded random subsets
of $k$ servers for twelve values of $k$ from 1 to 500. The random subsets are drawn uniformly
from all servers. Up to $k=20$ none of them holds any of the four servers the queries came from,
servers 0, 125, 250 and 375, and from $k=50$ on they hold up to four. The first $k$ servers always
include server 0.

Table~\ref{tab:nested} and Figure~\ref{fig:nested} give the result. At 128 probes recall moves
from 0.991 at two billion rows to 0.999 at a trillion, and at 256 probes from 0.999 to 1.000. At
narrow widths recall rises with the number of rows, at 16 probes from 0.797 to 0.952 and at 32
probes from 0.905 to 0.989. Between one and ten servers, where no random subset holds a server the
queries came from, recall at 32 probes already rises from 0.905 to 0.943, so the rise does not
depend on those servers. The rise is larger than the spread between subsets of one size. At 32
probes the five subsets of one server range from 0.898 to 0.908, of ten servers from 0.935 to
0.951, and of fifty from 0.960 to 0.980.

The corpus-row queries carry one more confound, since a subset that holds a query's own row
returns it as that query's nearest neighbour. The non-member queries are rows of no shard, so for
them no subset changes that, and their partials give the same series with no new scan
(Table~\ref{tab:nested}, last two columns). At 32 probes their recall rises from 0.843 at one server
to 0.969 at five hundred, with the five subsets of one server between 0.832 and 0.851, of ten
between 0.897 and 0.927 and of a hundred between 0.939 and 0.957. At 128 probes it rises from 0.980
to 0.999. Growing the corpus from two billion to a trillion rows therefore causes no measurable loss
of routed recall at any width under this protocol, for either query set, and at narrow widths it
raises it. The rise at 32 probes across the separate runs, 0.979, 0.978, 0.982, 0.983 and 0.989,
is the same effect with the confound present.

The nested series shows what happened. Whether a designer could have known it in advance is a
separate question, and we tested it before the non-member run was scored. Two registrations, each
committed to the repository with its models, its selection rule, its pass rule and its grader
script, predicted that run's routed recall at $10^{12}$ rows (Section~\ref{sec:repro}). Each
model maps a calibration curve, recall $R$ at row counts $N_1 < \dots < N_c$, to a prediction at
$N^{*} = 10^{12}$ as follows.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:m0}
\mathrm{M0}\quad \hat R(N^{*}) = R(N_c)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:m1}
\mathrm{M1}\quad 1 - \hat R(N) = D\,(N/N_c)^{\beta}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:m2}
\mathrm{M2}\quad \hat R(N) = \min\bigl(1,\; R_c + \gamma \log_{10}(N/N_c)\bigr)
\end{equation}
M0 carries the largest calibration size unchanged. M1 is a power law in the deficit, fitted as
$\log(1-R)$ linear in $\log N$, and M2 is recall linear in $\log N$, capped at 1. Both are least
squares fits over all the calibration sizes, so $D$ and $R_c$ are fitted values at $N_c$, not
measured ones. Table~\ref{tab:transfer} gives each registration's calibration sizes, its curve and
the fitted parameters of its registered models, from which the predictions can be refitted, and
\texttt{figdata/transfer\_fits.txt} gives every model at every width, refitted from the two
committed prediction records. Each registration chose its model per
width on the corpus-row queries, already scored, by the smallest error at $10^{12}$. The first
calibrated on nested subsets of one to twenty servers of this index, from $2\times10^{9}$ to
$4\times10^{10}$ rows, computed from those twenty servers' partials alone and committed before
the score. The second calibrated on separately built corpora of $10^{8}$ to $10^{9}$ rows, shards
of this index rebuilt from their seeds with its basis, coarse quantizer and router.

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} gives the grades against the measured 0.969 and 0.999. The first
registration passed at both widths. At 32 probes its fit on at most four percent of the index
predicted 0.953, and carrying the twenty-server value unchanged would have predicted 0.914. The
second failed its primary test. Its development comparison chose M2 at 32 probes because the cap
at 1 sat close to the corpus-row value of 0.989, a risk its registration stated. Its fitted line
reaches 1.121 at $10^{12}$, so the cap set the prediction, and on the non-member queries a
prediction of 1 is 4.4 standard errors too high. Its M1 fit on the same small
corpora predicted 0.953, but M1 was not its registered model at that width, so we report that only
as context. At 128 probes both passed. For a designer this is one positive result and one negative
one: a deficit law fitted on subsets of the index itself carried to a corpus
twenty-five times larger,
and a model chosen on separate small corpora did not, as registered. One index, one generator and
100 queries bound both.

Every reference neighbour lives in one of the 2048 cells, and each query orders the cells by the
priority the router uses. Inside a probed cell the routed pass scores every row with the same
asymmetric distance the exact scan used, so a row gets the same score in both passes up to the
order of floating-point additions, and a reference neighbour is returned exactly when its cell is
probed, as long as that difference cannot move a row across the tenth score. The audit of the
archived partials (Section~\ref{sec:run}) measured both, within two limits. Of the 2.3 million rows
that appear in a server's reference partial and one of its routed partials, 74 percent have
bit-equal scores and the rest differ by at most 7 units in the last place, $4.2\times10^{-7}$. That
is the largest difference among rows both passes returned, an observation and not a bound over
every row a pass scores. The other side is the gap between a query's tenth and eleventh reference
scores. The merge of per-server top tens holds the exact eleventh only when the top ten spans at
least two servers, since then no server holds more than nine of them and the eleventh is in its own
server's top ten. Every non-member query's top ten spans two to ten servers, and their smallest gap
is $2.1\times10^{-6}$, with no ties. Of the corpus-row queries, 77 span two or more servers, with
smallest gap $1.6\times10^{-5}$. The other 23 have all ten neighbours on the server of their home
shard, whose eleventh row no partial holds, so for them the merged gap, $3.4\times10^{-4}$ or more,
is only an upper bound, and their margin is not certified (\texttt{fleet\_boundary\_span.py}).
Recall at width $p$ is the share of
reference neighbours whose cell has rank below $p$ in its query's order. That identity, stated over
finite sets with exhaustive in-cell scoring as its hypothesis, is checked in Lean 4 with Mathlib in
the repository (\texttt{lean/Pvldb1t/Reachability.lean}, theorem \texttt{recall\_eq\_share}). The
proof establishes the set relationship under its hypotheses and nothing more. It takes equal
scores as a hypothesis. The measured margin carries it to the non-member queries and to 77 of the
corpus-row queries, as far as the observed score difference bounds the true one. For the other 23
the evidence is the agreement with the measured recall below, not a certificate. It needs the
reference neighbours, so it does not predict which rows become neighbours as the corpus grows, and
it says nothing about other corpora or query populations.

What it gives is an instrument and a check. The share needs the cell of each of the 1000 reference
neighbours and nothing else, so it was computed from the index metadata in 500 jobs of half a
minute each and one merge, with no distance scan. Table~\ref{tab:reach} and
Figure~\ref{fig:reach} give it at nine widths. It agreed with the two widths already measured, 32
and 128, to the last digit. We then recorded the values at 16, 64 and 256, submitted a routed pass
at those widths on all 500 servers, and the measured recall came back 0.952, 0.997 and 1.000, equal
to the recorded values at every width. That agreement validates the implementation of the routed
pass, the merge and the census against one another. The neighbours' cell ranks have median 1,
ninetieth percentile 9, ninety-ninth percentile 32 and maximum 169, so the one neighbour that 128
probes miss sits in the 170th cell of its query's order.

The identity also frames the stability across scale. The corpus grows by adding shards under one
shared coarse quantizer, so an added shard puts rows into the same 2048 cells and leaves every
query's probe order unchanged. Recall at width $p$ changes only if the ten nearest rows move to
cells of different rank. Nothing forces nearer rows into earlier cells, since a row near a cell
boundary can be close to the query and sit in a cell the priority ranks late. Whether they do is an
empirical question. By the identity, recall at width $p$ is the share of reference neighbours in
the first $p$ cells, so the nested series measures that share as rows are added, and on this index
it rises at narrow widths and does not fall at any width. That is an observation about this corpus
and router, not a consequence of the identity, and a corpus whose new rows land near cell
boundaries could behave otherwise.

The router adjusts each centroid angle by half the cell's radius, and a reader can ask whether the
result depends on that choice. The identity answers it without a scan. The census holds the cell of
every reference neighbour and the size of every cell, so for any cell order and any budget of rows,
recall is the share of neighbours whose cells fit in the budget. We gave each query, at each width,
the rows the fleet's order scored there and let three orders spend them: plain centroid order, the
fleet's order with half the radius, and the full radius. The fleet's order at its own budget

non-members'.

\subsection{The cells, query placement, and queries outside the index}
\label{sec:follow}

The same 500 metadata jobs took a census of the cells. The 2048 cells hold between 49.2 million
and 2.15 billion rows, with mean 488 million and median 418 million, a Gini coefficient of 0.328,
the largest one percent of cells holding 3.7 percent of rows, and no empty cell.

The 1000 reference neighbours of the corpus-row queries sit on 233 of the 500 servers, and one
server holds 16.6 percent of them, because each shard's own basis keeps a query's neighbours near
its home shard. Losing one server at random would cost 0.002 of recall in expectation, and losing
that one server would cost 0.166. Because every query is a corpus row, it is also its own nearest
neighbour.

The non-member set removes both. Its reference scan, routed passes at 32 and 128 probes and exact
merge were rerun over all 500 servers with the programs of the first run. Its 1000 reference
neighbours sit on 404 of the 500 servers, no server holds more than 1.0 percent of them, and the
median query has its ten neighbours on eight servers. Routed recall against the exact scan was
0.969 at 32 probes and 0.999 at 128, with 95 percent bootstrap intervals of 0.954 to 0.982 and 0.997
to 1.000. At 32 probes 78 of the 100 queries had recall 1.0 and the lowest had 0.6. At 128 probes
the two sets agree. At 32 probes the queries the index does not contain lose 0.020. A corpus row
has two advantages, its own row among its ten neighbours and neighbours in the cells its own
shard's basis favours, but the two sets also draw different subspaces, and with four subspaces per
set the causes cannot be told apart. Section~\ref{sec:regen} measures the gap on two hundred
subspaces.

\subsection{Two hundred queries from two hundred shards}
\label{sec:regen}

The corpus-row and non-member sets each take their 100 queries from four shards, so each samples
four of the generator's subspaces, and a bootstrap over their queries cannot show how recall varies
across subspaces. The many-shard set takes 200 queries, one from each of 200 shards, so it samples
two hundred subspaces. Half are corpus rows from shards on 100 different servers and half come from
seeds no corpus shard uses. By the time it was drawn the 500 index volumes had been released. The corpus is
a seed, so we measured it on a regenerated index. Each of 500 jobs regenerates its server's 400
shards one at a time into scratch space with the build's own code and the bootstrap's basis,
assigns the rows to cells with the global coarse quantizer and adopts the bootstrap's radii, runs
the exact scan and the routed search at 32 and 128 probes on that shard, keeps the best ten per
query, and discards the shard. Merging top-ten lists across shards is exact, so the result equals
a search of the whole server. Every regenerated shard file and its two cell files are hashed and
compared with the sha256 the first run recorded for the released index. The shard file itself
cannot match, because the index writes its creation time into the file, so its codes are checked
through the scores instead. A shard counts as
reproduced when both its cell files hash equal to that record and every neighbour it holds from the
released exact top tens of the two earlier sets comes back with its released float32 score bit for
bit. The build's floating-point results depend on the BLAS kernel the node selects, the thread count
and whether the shard was written whole or streamed in bands, and none of these was recorded per
shard, so each shard was rebuilt under the settings the first build could have used until it
reproduced, 1.12 builds per shard on average on the cluster. A shard written whole needs more than
two gigabytes to rebuild. Those shards ran at one CPU and three gigabytes on cluster nodes with
AVX-512, or on a two-socket Haswell workstation.

Of the 200000 shards, 199600 reproduced, and the 400 on 33 servers that no build
reproduced are left out of the comparison, so the many-shard set is measured on a regenerated
subset of 998 billion rows. The reproduced shards hold 998073 released neighbours
and the left-out shards 12. Of the left-out shards, 291 were also written whole on
AVX-512 nodes under all 31 settings. The passes that did so reproduced 90 of the 381 shards
they reached, all on servers 0 to 7, the eight built first, and none of the 196 they reached
on other servers. The other 109, on 13 servers, were each rebuilt at least
27 times on the cluster and the workstation but were not written whole on an AVX-512 node
under all 31 settings before submission. No reference neighbour of a corpus-row query lies in a left-out shard, so for
that set the comparison is exact. One reference neighbour of one non-member query does, and the
other 99 queries give 0.978 at 32 probes and 1.000 at 128. Sixty shards reproduce under
either kind of write and are included.\footnote{For these shards a whole write and a streamed one
both pass every check, so the record cannot say which the first build used. Either way the rebuilt
cell files and released scores equal the first run's.}

On the regenerated 998-billion-row subset the many-shard set's 100 corpus rows give 0.982 at 32
probes and
0.999 at 128, with 95 percent intervals of 0.969 to 0.993 and 0.997 to 1.000, and its 100 non-members give
0.977 and 1.000, with 0.967 to 0.986 at 32 probes. At 32 probes 89 corpus-row queries and
81 non-member queries had recall 1.0. The corpus-row neighbours sit on 283 servers, the
median query's on three, and the non-member neighbours on 395, the median query's on
eight. At 32 probes each four-shard value lies inside the many-shard set's interval for the
same kind of query. Every corpus-row query is still its own neighbour, yet at 32 probes the
corpus rows lead by 0.005, less than half the width of either interval. On two hundred
subspaces this measurement cannot tell a corpus row's two advantages from none at this width,
and since each four-shard value lies inside the many-shard interval for its kind, the four-shard
gap of 0.020 is consistent with the choice of four subspaces alone.

storage and serves 100 queries at once.

scale point and every subset of Section~\ref{sec:nested} uses it. We measured no other cell count. With the
cells fixed, the mean cell grows with the corpus, from about 49 thousand rows at $10^{8}$ to 488
million at $10^{12}$, and the work at a fixed width grows with it. At 128 probes a query scores 72
billion rows, 7 percent of the index, and the routed job's median time is half the exact scan's
(Table~\ref{tab:cost}). More cells would cut that work. With 65536 balanced cells, 128 probes would
score about 2 billion rows at $10^{12}$ instead of 62.5 billion. But smaller cells split a query's
reference neighbours over more of them, and by the identity of Section~\ref{sec:reach} recall at
width $p$ is the share of those neighbours whose cells come in the first $p$ of the probe order. How
that share falls at another cell count, and so what width the same recall would need, this run does
not measure. The claim of this paper is therefore narrower than a speed claim. With the cells and
the width fixed, routed recall against the exact scan does not degrade as the rows grow. It is not a
statement about latency, and not a claim that routed search at this cell count is much faster than
a parallel exact scan.

\section{How the measurement ran}
\label{sec:run}

\paragraph{Jobs.} Every job in the build and the measurement requested one CPU and two gigabytes
of memory, the cluster's smallest class, except 164 exact scans submitted in an earlier session
at six CPUs and eight gigabytes, and the rebuilds of whole-written shards in the regenerated-index
run of Section~\ref{sec:regen}, at one CPU and three gigabytes. The pool resumed at one CPU, kept those 164 partials and scanned

so six cores bought about seven percent on a scan that holds one. A scan over memory-mapped shards fits two gigabytes once
three choices are made. The queries come from the cache, because deriving them from the seed

shards is 5.8 GB. Scoring proceeds in blocks of 65536 rows. A build job streams its shards from
the seed in row bands and drops each written band from the page cache, because written pages count
against the job's memory limit. Table~\ref{tab:budget} gives what one job of each kind used.

\paragraph{Submission.} The driver publishes each job to a controller \cite{natsbursting} that
creates it only while the namespace has fewer than five jobs pending and twenty running and the
cluster has fewer than a hundred pending pods, and retries a deferred job after waits that double
from thirty seconds to fifteen minutes, for up to fifteen attempts, following an earlier design for
polite submission to shared clusters \cite{politesubmit}. Above the controller a pool keeps at most
twenty jobs in flight with no wave barrier, checkpoints each phase to a state file, adopts jobs
still on the cluster after a restart, recycles a failed job after a back-off from two minutes to
thirty, and waits ninety minutes for a submitted job to appear before it treats the job as lost,
because a job the controller has deferred is not yet visible and resubmitting it would queue a
duplicate. A separate analysis \cite{politeburst} studies such a gate when the capacity record it
reads may be stale, with a budget on jobs admitted while the cluster has no room.

\paragraph{Safety of repeated jobs.} Every job checks for its output and exits if it is there,
writes its output under a temporary name and renames it into place. A duplicate, a retry and a job adopted after a restart therefore
compute the same partial and either find it done or replace it whole. Two cases are weaker than
that, and we state them. The temporary name was per output and not per attempt, and Kubernetes
allows only one job of a name, but a pod can outlive its job on a node that no longer responds, so
two live writers for one server were not excluded by construction. And the score phase loads every
partial it finds, and the figure script asserts 500 per phase, but nothing checks shapes or
duplicate files. The
regenerated-index run of Section~\ref{sec:regen} writes under names unique to each attempt and
loads every existing output before it treats the output as done.

After the runs we audited the archived partials of both query sets with a script in the
repository (\texttt{fleet\_partials\_audit.py}). It checks that each phase has exactly one partial
for each of the 500 servers and no stray or temporary file beside them, that every partial holds
ids and scores of shape 100 by 10 in query-file order, that every id lies in its server's row
range and none repeats within a query, that scores are finite and non-increasing, that a row in
both a server's reference and routed partials has the same score up to floating-point reordering,
that no routed score exceeds the reference score of the same rank by more than that reordering
difference, and that the merged reference
top-10 of every query has no tie at its boundary and a gap there wider than twice the largest
score difference. That merged gap is the exact gap only where the top ten spans two or more
servers, which holds for every non-member query and 77 corpus-row queries
(Section~\ref{sec:reach}). Both runs pass, 3000 partials of the corpus-row run and 1500 of the non-member
run, the latter also against the sha256 of every archived file. Partials record no query
identifier, so query order is checked by shape against the shared query file and not row by row.

\paragraph{Failures.} Table~\ref{tab:pool} gives what the pool did. Two failure classes dominated.
One node returned a storage read fault to every job placed on it within 3 to 13 minutes of start,
and retries landed elsewhere. Pods pending past 45 minutes were recycled in the first run, and in
every instance checked the original pod had run and the retry found its output within minutes. In
the second sweep that rule was replaced by waiting, because a running job is never deleted on a
namespace other groups share. Six pods left on nodes that no longer responded were removed by
hand across the four days. Six index volumes became unreachable when their node went offline, and
each was recreated and rebuilt from its seed in 2 hours 32 minutes to 3 hours 49 minutes. No backup
of the index was kept.

during it. The 164 exact scans at six CPUs account for 145.6 of the scan's 417.8 hours, so the
scan held 1146 core-hours over its search calls and the whole corpus-row measurement 2077, and
those figures leave out each pod's start and the time between its search calls, which the record
does not hold. The third is what the jobs used. One sampled job of each kind averaged 0.88 cores

duration. The build is accounted separately. The six servers rebuilt in September took 2 hours 32
minutes to 3 hours 49 minutes each at one CPU, and the build pool of August and September kept no
per-server log, so its total is not in the record. Storage is 24.0 TB of index on 30.1 TB of
provisioned block volumes.

The regenerated-index run of Section~\ref{sec:regen} is accounted from its merge record, which
keeps each job's time in its shard loop, regeneration, hashing, checks and search together,
summed over the attempts that resumed it. Its 581 completed cluster jobs, 499 that each rebuilt
and searched one server and 82 that rebuilt shards on AVX-512 nodes, spent 4984 hours there, a
median of 9.0 hours per server. All ran at one CPU, and all at two gigabytes except 73
whole-write rebuild jobs at three. That is more than twice the search time of the two earlier
query sets together, because this run rebuilds the index, 1.12 builds per shard, where they
only read it. It is a one-time cost of checking the index by rebuilding it, with the third query
set measured in the same jobs, and not a cost of search. The drivers logged 677 submissions for those 581 jobs. The workstation's 46
partials add 439 hours of process time, at one to three threads a process, so they are not
core-hours. The run kept no copy of the index, since each job built one shard at a time into
scratch space and discarded it.

The routed times are not the latency of inverted-file search, and their ratio to the scan time is
not a speedup of the method. Each routed job opens its 400 shards from network block storage and
reads the probed cells for 100 queries at once, and Section~\ref{sec:work} gives the work. The tails
are node variance. Between 4 and 30 servers per phase took more than twice the median, the 16308 s
maximum of the scan is one server on one node that also produced the slowest routed pass, and in
every phase the ninetieth percentile is within 1.6 times the median.

It is a statement about this generator. A seeded corpus is what made a trillion-row index
buildable and checkable here. Every shard is a function of its seed, so the build moved no corpus
bytes, 199600 of the 200000 released shards were rebuilt with both cell files equal to the
released ones by sha256 and every released neighbour's score reproduced bit for bit
(Section~\ref{sec:regen}), and float rows could be recovered for the rerank bound below. Those
checks establish that the rebuilt shards assign every row to the released cells and score the
released neighbours as the released index did. They do not compare the shard files themselves,
whose hash differs by the creation time they record. A real corpus at this size would
first have to be embedded and stored, four petabytes of float vectors at 1024 dimensions. What the
generator decides is where a query's reference neighbours fall. By the identity of
Section~\ref{sec:reach}, recall at a width is the share of those neighbours whose cells the router
reaches first. On this corpus a corpus-row query's neighbours cluster near its home shard
(Section~\ref{sec:follow}), and every shard is a subspace of dimension 16, with hubness that rises
with the rows (Section~\ref{sec:system}). The real embeddings we measured have an intrinsic
dimension of 52 to 61 and less hubness growth, and could spread a query's neighbours over more cells, so that the same width
reaches fewer of them and the rise with the rows of Section~\ref{sec:nested} is smaller or absent.
What does not depend on the generator is the exactness of the merge, the identity as a relation
between sets, the accounting of work from cell sizes, and the machinery of Section~\ref{sec:run}.

The real-data pilot does not close that gap, and we do not combine it with the trillion-row result
into a claim about real data at $10^{12}$ rows. It is one corpus at one size, 15 million Cohere
Embed v3 vectors of 1024 dimensions, built with the same software but projected to 256 dimensions
with 4096 cells, where the fleet uses 24 and 2048, and scored on 500 queries. There the routed
shortlist returned 0.949 of the compressed scan at 64 probes and 0.990 at 256. That shows the
router reaching the exact scan's neighbours on one real corpus. It says nothing about how recall on
real embeddings moves as the rows grow. A scale series on real embeddings, or a second generator
with their intrinsic dimension and hubness, is the test that would, and we ran neither.

It is not a statement about recall against the truth. The compressed scan at $10^{9}$ rows found
0.592 of the float neighbours, and on the 15-million-row real corpus the compressed index found
0.693 of them by exact scan, 0.689 by routing at 256 probes, and 0.981 after reranking the routed
shortlist against stored originals. Four bits per dimension at this rate loses neighbours that only
a rerank tier recovers, and the trillion-row corpus, at intrinsic dimension 16 against a real
embedding's 52 to 61, cannot show that.

A cold store of originals at $10^{12}$ rows would be 128 TB of float vectors, so true recall is
established at $10^{9}$ and at the real pilot, not here. The seeded corpus does allow one bound.
The float rows named by a shortlist can be regenerated from the seed, so the compressed top-10 of
each query can be reranked by float cosine within the shortlist that contains it, and the share of
the compressed top-10 that survives that rerank is an upper bound on compressed-only true recall,
because a corpus float neighbour that lies in the shortlist is a shortlist float neighbour. The set
inequality behind the bound is checked in Lean 4 with Mathlib
(\texttt{lean/Pvldb1t/RerankBound.lean}, theorem \texttt{rerank\_upper\_bound}), under the same
caveat as the identity, that the proof covers the set relationship and not the data.
Table~\ref{tab:rerank} gives the result on 11002 regenerated rows. Of the reference scan's
compressed top-10, 0.621 survives the float rerank of its own shortlist on average, with 22 of 100
queries keeping all ten and a minimum of 0.100, so compressed-only true recall at $10^{12}$ rows is
at most 0.621 on this corpus. At one billion rows the cold store gave 0.592, inside this bound. The
routed shortlists behave the same way, 0.623 at 16 probes and 0.621 at 256. After reranking, the
routed top-10 agrees with the reranked reference top-10 at 0.940 for 16 probes, 0.982 for 32, 0.996
for 64 and 1.000 from 128 on, within 0.012 of the recall before reranking at every width. That
agreement is between two reranked shortlists of width 100, each a restricted candidate set, and it
is not float recall over the corpus.

\section{Related work}

Product quantization with asymmetric distance computation and inverted files
\cite{jegou2011product} is the basis of the index, and the two-stage arrangement in which a
compressed scan produces a shortlist that a finer code or the original vectors rerank dates from the
same group's billion-vector work \cite{jegou2011rerank}. Locally optimized product quantization
\cite{kalantidis2014lopq} learns a separate rotation and codebook for each cell, the opposite choice
to the shared basis we use to keep scores comparable across shards, and anisotropic quantization
\cite{guo2020scann} and RaBitQ \cite{gao2024rabitq} are the current forms of the quantizer with, in
the latter case, an error bound on the estimated distance. Locally adaptive scalar quantization
\cite{aguerrebere2023lvq} puts a compressed representation under a graph index and reranks from a
second tier, the arrangement our $10^{9}$ and real-embedding figures use. Locality-sensitive hashing
\cite{indyk1998lsh} and navigable graphs \cite{malkov2020hnsw} are the usual alternatives to the
inverted file and are not what this paper measures.

Billion-scale search on one machine or one node's disk is established
\cite{johnson2019faiss,subramanya2019diskann,chen2021spann}, and the NeurIPS'21 Big ANN competition
\cite{simhadri2022bigann} fixed the datasets and the reporting for that scale, with its 2023
successor \cite{simhadri2024bigann23} turning to filtered, sparse, out-of-distribution and
streaming search at ten million rows. Distributed vector databases such as Milvus
\cite{wang2021milvus} and its cloud-native successor \cite{guo2022manu} shard an index across nodes
and merge per-node results, the arrangement our fleet uses without a serving layer, recent work
places a billion-scale index on distributed storage \cite{yu2025dsann}, and a single graph index
has been spread over more than a thousand machines at $5 \times 10^{10}$ vectors
\cite{adams2025distributedann}.

The closest precedents are at our scale. The Faiss library paper \cite{douze2024faiss} indexes 1.5
trillion 144-dimensional vectors, compressed to 54 bytes each, built as 2000 shards on a few hundred
servers and regrouped into 100 indexes by inverted list, memory-mapped on one machine and searched
through 20 intermediate servers in about a second per query. It reports the construction and the
search time and no recall at that scale. Its Section 5.1 gives the argument for why recall at a
fixed probe count should not fall as a fixed-quantizer index grows, with evidence on up to a billion
vectors in the regime of more cells than the data needs, and our nested series is a measurement of
that argument at a trillion rows in the opposite regime. UBASE \cite{tian2026ubase} serves about a trillion vectors
at ByteDance and reports latency and memory for that deployment and recall on sets of up to about a
hundred million vectors. Both are systems that run at a trillion vectors. This paper is a
measurement at a trillion rows. We found no published comparison of a routed index against the
exact scan of the same index at that scale, no scale series inside one index, and no record from
which such numbers can be recomputed.

\section{Conclusion}

A trillion-row compressed index was scanned exactly and searched by routing over the same shards.
On this seeded corpus, of intrinsic dimension 16, scaling from $10^{8}$ to $10^{12}$ rows causes no measurable loss of routed
recall against the exact scan for corpus-row queries, inside the one index recall at narrow widths rises with the number of
rows, and queries the index does not contain give 0.969 at 32 probes and 0.999 at 128.
Two hundred queries from two hundred shards, measured on the regenerated 998-billion-row subset, give
0.982 and 0.977 at 32 probes and 0.999 and 1.000 at 128, so the result does not rest on four
of the generator's subspaces. Recall at any width is the share of reference neighbours whose cell the
router reaches, a count that matched five measured widths, and by that count plain centroid order
does as well as the router's own at equal work. The work that buys the recall is a fixed share of
the index per query, a median of 19 billion rows at 32 probes, so a width tuned small held as the
corpus grew, and bounding the work would take more cells, which score fewer rows per probe and
may need a different width, a trade-off this run did not measure. A registered fit on twenty servers
predicted the non-member recall at $10^{12}$ rows within error, and a registered fit on separate
small corpora failed at 32 probes. Against float rows
regenerated from the seed, the compressed ranking keeps at most 0.621 of the true neighbours. On
real embeddings the result is tested at one size only, 15 million rows, where 256 probes returned
0.990 of the compressed scan. The
build and the measurement of the released index ran in a shared cluster's smallest job class,
except 164 exact scans at six CPUs (Section~\ref{sec:run}), and
all of it can be recomputed from the seed and the archived record.
